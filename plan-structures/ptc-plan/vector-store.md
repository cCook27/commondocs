## Premium Tax Credit Plan
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Premium Tax Credit Plan

> **The Premium Tax Credit Plan is a benefits strategy for non-ALE employers that preserves employees' access to Marketplace premium tax credits while still providing meaningful, tax-advantaged employer benefits.**

Small employers often assume that being a good employer requires buying a small-group major-medical plan. That assumption can produce a startling result: the employer and employee replace federal premium-tax-credit dollars with their own dollars, frequently to buy substantially similar coverage from a smaller set of options.

CommonCare's experience is that households can unknowingly walk away from thousands of dollars per year. A useful planning benchmark is **about $5,000 per household per year**, but the actual premium tax credit (PTC) can be much smaller or much larger because it depends on household income, household composition, ages, location, the local second-lowest-cost silver plan (SLCSP), and the coverage selected.

The plan is not a reimbursement arrangement for individual insurance. It is a coordinated structure in which:

1. The employer does **not** offer affordable minimum-value major-medical coverage that would block PTC eligibility;
2. Eligible employees independently enroll in qualified health plans through the Marketplace and claim the PTC;
3. The employer may offer a carefully designed package of excepted benefits, HSA contributions, CommonFunds, and taxable compensation; and
4. CommonCare administers the employer benefits, Marketplace comparison, employee elections, and payroll flows without treating employee-selected Marketplace coverage as employer sponsored.

> [!IMPORTANT]
> The point is not to exploit a drafting gap. Congress applied the employer shared-responsibility rules to applicable large employers and created income-based credits for eligible households buying Marketplace coverage. Non-ALE employers are outside §4980H, and federal rules expressly preserve PTC eligibility where an employer offer is not affordable and minimum value, where an employee does not enroll in employer-sponsored MEC, and where employer benefits are genuinely excepted.

## The dollars should be compared before the coverage channel is chosen
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part I — The economic decision > The dollars should be compared before the coverage channel is chosen

For an eligible household, the PTC is federal money available only for qualified health plans purchased through a Marketplace. It is not available for a small-group policy merely because the group policy and Marketplace policy have similar benefits, networks, or actuarial value.

That makes the first comparison economic:

```text
net Marketplace cost
= selected Marketplace premium
− allowed premium tax credit
```

versus:

```text
net group-plan cost
= employer premium contribution
+ employee premium contribution
```

The employer contribution is still compensation cost. Moving a dollar from wages, bonuses, retirement, HSA funding, or other benefits into group insurance does not make the dollar free. If a Marketplace tax credit would have purchased the same risk protection, replacing it dollar-for-dollar with employer and employee money should be an explicit decision—not an accidental consequence of conventional plan design.

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.what-the-ptc-actually-pays -->
## What the PTC actually pays
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part I — The economic decision > What the PTC actually pays

The PTC generally equals the lesser of:

- The premium for the qualified health plan in which the household enrolls; or
- The local SLCSP premium for the coverage family minus the household's expected contribution.

The expected contribution is household income multiplied by the applicable percentage for the household's federal-poverty-level band. The SLCSP is therefore a benchmark, not a required plan selection. A household may apply the credit to another eligible bronze, silver, gold, or platinum qualified health plan. [IRS Publication 974, “Monthly credit amount”](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p974#en_US_2025_publink100023185) · [26 U.S.C. §36B](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?req=granuleid:USC-prelim-title26-section36B&num=0&edition=prelim)

### Illustration 3 — paying twice for substantially equal protection

The regulation states that employer-sponsored coverage generally counts against PTC eligibility when it is affordable and provides minimum value. But once an individual **enrolls** in eligible employer-sponsored coverage, the affordability and minimum-value exceptions no longer apply. [26 CFR §1.36B-2(c)(3)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/1.36B-2) · [IRS Publication 974, “Employer-Sponsored Plans”](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p974#en_US_2025_publink100023213)

For 2026, the employee affordability threshold under §36B is **9.96% of household income**. Family-member affordability is tested using the employee's required contribution for the family members offered coverage, not merely the self-only employee rate. [IRS PTC Questions and Answers](https://www.irs.gov/affordable-care-act/individuals-and-families/questions-and-answers-on-the-premium-tax-credit) · [IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-25, §3.02](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-32_IRB#REV-PROC-2025-25)

## Why non-ALE status matters
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part II — The PTC eligibility rule > Why non-ALE status matters

An employer is generally an applicable large employer for a calendar year if it averaged at least 50 full-time employees, including full-time equivalents, during the preceding calendar year. Controlled-group aggregation, seasonal-worker rules, and changes in workforce size can affect the determination. #### ALE

- Context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part II — The PTC eligibility rule > Why non-ALE status matters
- Employer status: ALE
- Effect of a full-time employee receiving PTC: May trigger §4980H(a) or §4980H(b), depending on the employer's overall offer and the employee's facts


This is why the PTC Plan is principally a non-ALE strategy. A non-ALE can preserve PTC access without taking a §4980H penalty because §4980H does not apply to it.

An ALE may intentionally accept §4980H(b) exposure for particular full-time employees when the household PTC and workforce economics are compelling. That is a lawful financial tradeoff, but it requires careful modeling and administration. For 2026, the indexed §4980H(b) amount is **$5,010 annually per affected full-time employee**, assessed monthly and subject to the statutory calculation and cap. [IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-26](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-33_IRB#REV-PROC-2025-26) · [26 CFR §54.4980H-5](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/54.4980H-5)

> [!IMPORTANT]
> “Non-ALE” is not synonymous with “fewer than 50 people on payroll today.” ALE status is a prior-year, full-time-plus-FTE, aggregated-employer calculation and must be confirmed each year.

## Dental and vision
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part III — Benefits an employer can still offer > Dental and vision

- Self-fund limited-scope dental or vision with separate administration and proper plan documents;
- Pay employer contributions tax-free under the applicable rules; and
- Use CommonCare to administer the benefit.

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.hsa-contributions -->
## HSA contributions
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part III — Benefits an employer can still offer > HSA contributions

An employee enrolled in an HSA-qualified Marketplace HDHP can remain eligible for employer HSA contributions if the employee has no disqualifying other coverage. For 2026, the HSA contribution limits are $4,400 for self-only coverage and $8,750 for family coverage. [IRS Revenue Procedure 2025-19](https://www.irs.gov/irb/2025-21_IRB#REV-PROC-2025-19)

Employer HSA contributions, and employee HSA contributions made through a §125 salary-reduction arrangement, are generally excluded from federal income-tax withholding, Social Security, Medicare, and FUTA when the contribution is reasonably believed excludable. [IRS Publication 15-B, “Health Savings Accounts”](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b#en_US_2026_publink1000193741) · [IRS Publication 15, “Health savings accounts”](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15)

The payroll-tax advantage should be stated precisely:

- The employer generally saves its **7.65%** Social Security and Medicare share;
- The employee generally saves the corresponding **7.65%** employee share;
- The combined ordinary FICA advantage is therefore **15.3%**, before wage-base and Additional Medicare Tax effects; and
- FUTA and, depending on state law and rating mechanics, state unemployment or workers' compensation payroll charges may also be reduced.

> [!WARNING]
> A general-purpose Health FSA or HRA usually makes an employee ineligible to contribute to an HSA. An HSA-compatible CommonFunds design must use limited-purpose or post-deductible coverage and satisfy the HSA rules. PTC compatibility and HSA compatibility are different questions. [IRS Publication 15-B](https://www.irs.gov/publications/p15b#en_US_2026_publink1000193716)

## CommonFunds plus MEC: threading the needle
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part III — Benefits an employer can still offer > CommonFunds plus MEC: threading the needle

[CommonFunds](../common-funds/human-readable.md) is an administrative experience, not a new legal benefit category. In the PTC Plan, each dollar must remain inside a valid excepted-benefit component.

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.why-the-mec-offer-exists -->
### Why the MEC offer exists
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part III — Benefits an employer can still offer > CommonFunds plus MEC: threading the needle > Why the MEC offer exists

Federal regulations impose an underlying-offer requirement:

- An excepted-benefit **Health FSA** requires other group health coverage, not limited to excepted benefits, to be made available for the year to the class of participants; and
- An **EBHRA** requires the same sponsor to make other group health coverage available to the participant that is neither limited to excepted benefits nor itself an HRA or other account-based group health plan.

[45 CFR §146.145(b)(3)(v) and (viii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/45/146.145) · [26 CFR §54.9831-1(c)(3)(v) and (viii)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/54.9831-1)

CommonCare's [Self-Funded Basic MEC Plan](../self-funded-mec/basic-mec/human-readable.md) is designed to supply that non-excepted employer group-health-plan offer without providing minimum value.

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.offer-the-mec-do-not-enroll-the-ptc-claimant -->
### Offer the MEC; do not enroll the PTC claimant
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part III — Benefits an employer can still offer > CommonFunds plus MEC: threading the needle > Offer the MEC; do not enroll the PTC claimant

The two systems fit together because the excepted-benefit rules require the underlying plan to be **made available**, while the PTC rules distinguish an offer from enrollment:

1. The employer offers the self-funded MEC to the eligible class.
2. The employee who intends to claim PTC declines the MEC.
3. Because the MEC does not provide minimum value, its offer does not by itself make the employee ineligible for PTC.
4. The employee enrolls independently in a Marketplace qualified health plan.
5. The employee may participate in the properly designed excepted-benefit FSA and/or EBHRA because the employer made the underlying MEC available.

> [!DANGER]
> The employee seeking PTC must not enroll in the MEC. Employer-sponsored self-funded MEC is minimum essential coverage. Enrollment—not merely an affordable-MV offer—makes the enrolled employee ineligible for PTC for the same months. CommonCare must preserve a clear opt-out and prevent automatic enrollment from silently destroying the credit.

## Unrestricted wages versus an employer payment plan
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part IV — Payroll and taxable allowances > Unrestricted wages versus an employer payment plan

## A permissible split allowance
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part IV — Payroll and taxable allowances > A permissible split allowance

An employer may define a $300 monthly compensation package, for example, as:

- **$150 restricted benefit credit:** available only for qualified employer-sponsored benefits and forfeited if unused; and
- **$150 cashable flex credit:** available as taxable wages whether or not the employee buys any health coverage.

The restricted $150 can be use-it-or-lose-it because it never funds the Marketplace option. The cashable $150 can ultimately be spent on a Marketplace premium because it belongs to the employee as ordinary wages. CommonCare must keep the elections and payroll codes separate.

If cash eligibility is conditioned on participation in another employer benefit, that condition must be documented as part of the compensation/benefit design and tested under the rules applicable to that employer benefit. The cash itself still cannot be conditioned on purchasing or proving payment for Marketplace coverage.

# Part V — The CommonCare operating model
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part V — The CommonCare operating model

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.employer-experience -->
## Employer experience
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part V — The CommonCare operating model > Employer experience

The employer:

1. Confirms non-ALE status for the year;
2. Adopts the selected employer-sponsored benefits and governing documents;
3. Offers the self-funded MEC where needed to support CommonFunds excepted-benefit components;
4. Offers dental, vision, HSA funding, CommonFunds, and taxable compensation under their respective rules;
5. Does not sponsor, recommend, or reimburse Marketplace major-medical coverage; and
6. Uses CommonCare for plan administration, employee education, election records, and clean payroll classification.

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.employee-experience -->
## Employee experience
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part V — The CommonCare operating model > Employee experience

The employee:

1. Sees that the employer intentionally preserves access to the PTC—a material advantage of working for a non-ALE;
2. Receives a clear warning that accepting employer MEC will block PTC for enrolled months;
3. Declines MEC when choosing the PTC pathway;
4. Shops the Marketplace and compares plans through CommonCare's independent analytical tools;
5. Enrolls in the employee's own qualified health plan;
6. Applies any available APTC and pays the remaining premium personally, directly or by post-tax payroll remittance;
7. Elects compatible employer benefits; and
8. Reports household changes to the Marketplace and reconciles APTC on the federal income-tax return.

<!-- record_id: product.ptc-plan.why-this-follows-the-regulatory-architecture -->
## Why this follows the regulatory architecture
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part V — The CommonCare operating model > Why this follows the regulatory architecture

This design respects each boundary rather than blending them:

- The Marketplace plan remains individual coverage selected by the employee;
- The PTC is determined under §36B;
- The non-ALE is not subject to §4980H;
- The MEC is a genuine employer group health plan, offered and administered as written;
- Employees claiming PTC genuinely decline the MEC;
- Dental, vision, Health FSA, and EBHRA components qualify separately as excepted benefits;
- CommonFunds does not reimburse individual major-medical premiums;
- HSA contributions are made only for HSA-eligible employees; and
- Any money usable for a Marketplace premium first becomes unconditional taxable wages.

The structure does not depend on relabeling a reimbursement, ignoring a plan, or arguing that form defeats substance. Its records should show the same facts its documents state.

## Employer checklist
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Part VI — Implementation controls > Employer checklist

- [ ] Determine ALE status annually, including controlled-group aggregation and full-time equivalents.
- [ ] Confirm the employer is not offering affordable minimum-value coverage to the PTC-eligible class.
- [ ] Adopt the MEC and excepted-benefit documents before the plan year.

- [ ] Separate restricted employer-benefit credits from unrestricted taxable wages.
- [ ] Apply the Health FSA maximum-benefit rule and the current EBHRA annual limit.
- [ ] Issue the required EBHRA notice and maintain SPD, claims, appeals, and substantiation procedures.
- [ ] Test HSA eligibility independently; use limited-purpose or post-deductible CommonFunds components where required.
- [ ] Review dental and vision for limited-scope excepted-benefit status.
- [ ] Train employer staff not to recommend Marketplace issuers or plans.

## Core principle
> Retrieval context: Premium Tax Credit Plan — Primary authorities > Core principle

> **A small employer should not spend employer and employee compensation to replace a premium tax credit by accident. Preserve the federal subsidy where the law provides it, then build real employer benefits around it without crossing the lines that make individual coverage employer sponsored or that make excepted benefits non-excepted.**
